The 50/30/20 Budget Rule: How to Use It (With Examples)

By YOUR NAME · Nov 20, 2026 · 6 min read

If you've never kept a budget, the hardest part is knowing where to start. The 50/30/20 rule gives you a simple starting point: split your after-tax income into three buckets and check how your spending compares.

What is the 50/30/20 rule?

The rule was popularized by Senator Elizabeth Warren and her daughter Amelia Warren Tyagi in the book All Your Worth. It divides take-home pay like this:

  • 50% for needs: housing, utilities, groceries, transportation, insurance, minimum debt payments.
  • 30% for wants: dining out, streaming, hobbies, travel, upgrades you could live without.
  • 20% for savings and extra debt payments: emergency fund, retirement, investing, paying debt beyond the minimum.

A worked example

Say your monthly take-home pay is $3,500.

BucketShareAmount
Needs50%$1,750
Wants30%$1,050
Savings / debt20%$700

If rent, food, transport and insurance already add up to $2,100, your needs are 60% of income. That's a signal to either trim costs or lower the wants bucket for now.

How to set it up in four steps

1. Calculate your after-tax income

Use what actually lands in your bank account each month, after taxes and payroll deductions. If your income varies, use the average of the last six months or your lowest recent month.

2. Track last month's spending

Go through your bank and card statements and label each transaction as a need, a want or savings. Be honest: a $90 phone plan is a need, a $150 phone plan with every add-on is partly a want.

3. Compare and adjust

Add up each bucket and compare it to the 50/30/20 targets. Most people find wants run higher than expected.

4. Automate the 20%

Set up automatic transfers to savings the day after payday. Money you never see is money you don't spend.

When the rule doesn't fit

In high-cost cities, needs can easily reach 60% or more. In that case a 60/20/20 or 60/10/30 split may be more realistic. If you carry high-interest debt, shift part of your wants into debt payments until it is gone. The percentages are a guide, not a law.

Common mistakes

  • Using gross income instead of take-home pay.
  • Counting subscriptions you forgot about as needs.
  • Treating savings as "whatever is left" rather than paying yourself first.

Bottom line

The 50/30/20 rule won't fit everyone perfectly, but it gives you a clear benchmark in minutes. Start with it, see where you land, and adjust the numbers to match your real life.
